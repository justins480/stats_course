Introduction to Statistics

A practical guide to identifying, comparing, and choosing sampling strategies.

What is Sampling?

Sampling is the process of selecting a subset of individuals or items from a larger population to gather information about the population. Instead of studying an entire population (which is often impossible or impractical), we study a sample and use it to make inferences about the population.

Why Sample?

  • Cost: Studying a sample is much cheaper than studying the entire population
  • Time: Sampling is faster than collecting data from everyone
  • Feasibility: Some populations are impossible to completely enumerate
  • Accuracy: Smaller samples often allow for more careful data collection

Two Main Categories of Sampling

Probability Sampling: Every member of the population has a known, non-zero chance of being selected. Results can be generalized to the population.

Non-Probability Sampling: Selection is not random. Some members have no chance of being selected. Results cannot be reliably generalized to the population.

Probability Sampling Methods

Random selection ensures each person has a known chance of being chosen. Results can be generalized to the population.

How It Works

  1. Obtain a complete list of all population members
  2. Assign each person a number
  3. Use a random number generator or random method to select your sample

✓ Advantages

  • Unbiased
  • Reduces selection bias
  • Easy to understand
  • Can generalize results

✗ Disadvantages

  • Requires complete population list
  • Can be expensive
  • Time-consuming for large populations
  • May not represent minority groups
Real-World Example: A sociologist randomly selects 500 households from the city's residential address database to study neighborhood social cohesion.

When to Use

Use simple random sampling when you have a manageable population list and need an unbiased, representative sample.

How It Works

  1. Calculate k = Population size / Desired sample size
  2. Randomly select a starting point (between 1 and k)
  3. Select every kth item after that

✓ Advantages

  • Easy to implement
  • No random number generator needed
  • Works well for ordered lists
  • Can generalize results

✗ Disadvantages

  • Can introduce bias if list has pattern
  • Requires population list
  • May miss important groups
Real-World Example: A researcher surveys every 20th person entering a public transit station to examine commuting patterns.

When to Use

Use systematic sampling when you have a list and want a simpler alternative to simple random sampling.

How It Works

  1. Identify important characteristics that vary (age, gender, income, etc.)
  2. Divide population into non-overlapping groups (strata)
  3. Randomly sample from within each stratum
  4. Combine samples from all strata

✓ Advantages

  • Ensures representation of all groups
  • More precise estimates
  • Can generalize to population
  • Better for diverse populations

✗ Disadvantages

  • Requires knowing population characteristics
  • More complex to implement
  • Deciding strata can be subjective
Real-World Example: A sociologist divides residents into age groups and randomly samples from each group to examine attitudes toward policing.

When to Use

Use stratified sampling when the population has distinct subgroups and you want to ensure all are represented.

  1. Divide population into naturally occurring clusters
  2. Randomly select some clusters
  3. Include ALL members from selected clusters in your sample

✓ Advantages

  • Very cost-effective
  • Easy to implement
  • Good for geographically scattered populations
  • Don't need complete population list

✗ Disadvantages

  • Less precise than stratified sampling
  • Clusters may not be representative
  • Cluster bias possible
Real-World Example: A researcher randomly selects 15 neighborhoods in a metropolitan area and interviews every household within those neighborhoods about community engagement.

When to Use

Use cluster sampling when the population is naturally grouped and data collection is logistically challenging.

Non-Probability Sampling Methods

Selection is not random. Cannot generalize results to the population with confidence.

✓ Advantages

  • Very quick and easy
  • Inexpensive
  • Good for preliminary research

✗ Disadvantages

  • Highly biased
  • Cannot generalize
  • Not representative
  • Often samples only "willing" participants
Real-World Example: A researcher surveys students sitting in the campus student union about social media use because they are easy to reach.

When to Use

Use convenience sampling only for exploratory research or when probability sampling is impossible.

How It Works

  1. Start with an initial participant
  2. Ask them to refer others who meet your criteria
  3. Interview those referrals
  4. Ask them to refer others, continuing the chain

✓ Advantages

  • Great for hard-to-reach populations
  • Builds trust and rapport
  • Efficient for sensitive topics

✗ Disadvantages

  • Highly biased
  • Cannot generalize
  • Similar people refer similar people
  • Takes time
Real-World Example: A researcher studying people experiencing homelessness begins with one participant, who refers others in their social network.

When to Use

Use snowball sampling for hard-to-reach or hidden populations where building trust is important.

How It Works

  1. Identify important characteristics (age, gender, income, etc.)
  2. Set quotas for each group (e.g., 40% men, 60% women)
  3. Recruit participants until quotas are met
  4. Within each group, selection is non-random (convenient)

✓ Advantages

  • Ensures representation of groups
  • Faster than stratified sampling
  • Cheaper than probability methods

✗ Disadvantages

  • Non-random within quotas introduces bias
  • Cannot generalize
  • Quotas may not reflect population accurately
Real-World Example: A researcher recruits participants until the sample includes equal numbers of men and women, selecting whoever is available within each group.

When to Use

Use quota sampling when you need group representation but can't use random selection.

  • Good for specific populations
  • Efficient use of resources
  • Finds "information-rich" cases

✗ Disadvantages

  • Heavily biased toward researcher's judgment
  • Cannot generalize
  • Subjective and prone to error
Real-World Example: A sociologist studying formerly incarcerated individuals intentionally recruits participants who have been released within the past two years.

When to Use

Use purposive sampling for qualitative research where you need specific types of participants.

✗ Disadvantages

  • Cannot generalize
  • Biased toward interested/opinionated people
Real-World Example: An online advertisement invites people to participate in a study about attitudes toward immigration. Only those who volunteer are included.

When to Use

Avoid this method for generalizable research. Use only when necessary and acknowledge the limitation.

Key Takeaways

  • Probability sampling uses randomization and can generalize to the population
  • Non-probability sampling is convenient but cannot reliably generalize
  • Choice of method depends on your research question, budget, population, and access
  • Better sampling methods (probability) give more trustworthy results, but are more expensive
  • Always disclose your sampling method so readers know the limitations
Remember: The best sampling method is the one that minimizes bias and best answers your research question, within your constraints.
